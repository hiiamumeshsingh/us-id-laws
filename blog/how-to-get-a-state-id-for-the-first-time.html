How to Get a State ID for the First Time

Understand the difference between REAL ID-compliant credentials and standard state-issued identification.

Last reviewed: September 2026

Getting your first state ID sounds simple until you start looking at the paperwork.

Then the questions show up: Which document proves who I am? Does a utility bill count? What if my name is different on my birth certificate? Do I need REAL ID? Do I have to make an appointment? How much will it cost?

The good news is that the process is usually straightforward once you know what your state wants. The annoying part is that there is no single nationwide application process. Your state decides the documents, fees, age rules and appointment process.

Quick answer: To get a state ID for the first time, you generally apply through your state's motor vehicle or driver's licensing agency, prove your identity and date of birth, provide any required Social Security and residency documents, pay the applicable fee and complete the photo and other identity-verification steps. If you want REAL ID, expect additional documentation.

First, make sure you actually need a state ID

A state ID is designed for identification. It does not give you permission to drive.

If you need a government-issued photo credential but do not drive, a state ID may be exactly what you are looking for. California, for example, describes its ID cards as documents used to prove identity or age and specifically notes that they do not allow the holder to operate a motor vehicle. citeturn0search3

If you need driving privileges, you are looking at a driver's license instead. If you only need identification, there is no reason to take a driving test just to obtain a photo ID.

Step 1: Find the right state agency

Start with the agency that issues driver's licenses and identification cards in the state where you live.

It may be called the DMV, Department of Driver Services, Department of Public Safety or something else. The name changes from state to state.

USAGov maintains a state-by-state directory of motor vehicle services, which is a useful starting point if you are not sure where to go. citeturn0search0

Do not use a random third-party "DMV" website to determine your requirements. Start with the official state site, because that is where the current fee, document list and appointment instructions will be published.

Step 2: Decide whether you want a REAL ID

This decision is worth making before you gather your documents.

A REAL ID is not a different kind of state ID in the everyday sense. It is a state-issued identification card that meets federal security standards.

USAGov says you can choose to make a state ID REAL ID-compliant when applying or renewing. The state determines the process and documents needed. citeturn0search1

If you expect to use your state ID for federal purposes covered by REAL ID, such as applicable domestic air travel, tell the agency you want a REAL ID and follow its document checklist.

If you do not need it, a standard state ID may still be available. The choice depends on your situation and your state's rules.

Step 3: Gather your identity documents

This is the part where applications tend to go wrong.

Your state will usually want an acceptable document that establishes your identity and date of birth. The exact list is different in each state.

Common examples include:

  • U.S. passport or passport card
  • Certified U.S. birth certificate
  • Permanent Resident Card
  • Certificate of Naturalization or Citizenship
  • Certain foreign passports and immigration documents

For REAL ID, USAGov gives examples including a U.S. birth certificate, U.S. passport or Permanent Resident Card. California's current REAL ID checklist similarly requires one qualifying identity document showing the applicant's date of birth and full name. citeturn0search1turn0search7

Do not assume that a photocopy, hospital souvenir birth certificate or expired document will be accepted just because it contains your name. Your state decides what qualifies.

For a deeper explanation of the difference between identity documents and other types of paperwork, see our guide to what counts as proof of identity.

Step 4: Make sure your name matches

A name mismatch is one of the easiest ways to turn a simple DMV visit into a second trip.

Imagine your birth certificate says one name, your Social Security record says another and your current documents use a third version. The state may require documents connecting those names.

Marriage certificates, divorce records and court orders can be used in some situations to establish a legal name change. USAGov recommends using certified copies of key documents when notifying government agencies about a name change. citeturn0search15

For REAL ID, California specifically says additional name-change documentation is required when the name on the identity document does not match the applicant's current true full name. citeturn0search10

If your documents do not match, solve that question before your appointment if you can.

Step 5: Bring your Social Security information

Many states require your Social Security number as part of the application.

That does not always mean the same physical document is required. Depending on the state and credential, acceptable evidence can include a Social Security card, W-2, pay stub or another approved record.

USAGov lists Social Security card, W-2 and pay stub examples for REAL ID applications, but the exact acceptable documents are set by the state. citeturn0search1

Check the official checklist rather than bringing whichever document happens to be easiest.

Step 6: Bring proof that you live in the state

Residency is separate from identity.

Your passport can prove who you are. It does not necessarily prove where you live now.

States may accept documents such as:

  • Utility bills
  • Bank statements
  • Lease or rental agreements
  • Insurance documents
  • Mortgage statements
  • Government correspondence
  • School or employment records

California's current REAL ID checklist, for example, requires two different printed documents showing the applicant's name and California mailing address. citeturn0search7

That is a California rule, not a nationwide requirement. Another state may ask for one document, two documents or a different combination altogether.

Our guide on why states ask for proof of residency explains why this is a separate part of the application.

Step 7: Check the fee before you go

State ID fees vary widely.

Some states charge a standard application fee. Others have reduced-fee or no-fee programs for particular applicants. Age, disability, veteran status, income or other eligibility categories can sometimes affect the price.

California, for example, currently lists regular, reduced-fee and no-fee ID categories. Georgia also has different ID fee categories. citeturn0search3turn0search9

Do not rely on a fee you saw in an old article. Check the state's current fee schedule before you leave home.

For more detail, see how much a state ID costs.

Step 8: Complete the application

The application process differs by state.

Some states let you start online. Others may require you to complete the entire application at an office. Even when an application can be started online, you may still have to appear in person to finish identity verification, have your photo taken or complete another required step.

California is one example. Its DMV allows applicants to start a driver's license or ID card application online, but the application is completed by visiting a DMV field office. citeturn0search8

Georgia's current process similarly asks applicants to submit information before visiting a Customer Service Center and then bring the required documents. citeturn0search9

So "apply online" does not necessarily mean "get your ID without visiting the DMV."

Step 9: Complete the in-person identity checks

At the office, expect some combination of document verification, photography, signature capture and other identity checks.

The exact process depends on the state.

California, for example, says applicants may have their thumbprint scanned and photo taken when applying for an ID card. citeturn0search3

These steps are part of the state's credential-issuance process. They are not extra tests you need to pass just because you are getting an ID instead of a driver's license.

What if this is your first-ever government photo ID?

That is not unusual.

You may have a birth certificate, Social Security documentation and school or medical records without ever having held a state ID.

The important thing is to work backward from the state's identity requirements. Do not worry that you need an existing driver's license to prove who you are. The purpose of the application is to establish your identity from acceptable source documents.

If you are missing a key document, solve that problem first. For example, if you need a certified birth certificate, you will generally need to request it from the vital records office in the state or territory where the birth occurred. USAGov provides separate guidance for replacing vital records. citeturn0search4

What if you are under 18?

Age rules vary by state.

Some states issue identification cards to children and teenagers, while others have different requirements for minors, including parent or guardian involvement.

California, for example, says its DMV may issue an ID card to a person of any age. citeturn0search3

Do not assume another state's rules are the same. If you are applying for a minor, check the specific state's page for age, consent, documentation and appointment requirements.

What if you recently moved to the state?

This is where residency documentation becomes especially important.

If you recently moved, you may not have a lease, utility bill or bank statement showing the new address yet. Some states accept alternative documents, while others have specific rules for people who are new residents.

Do not manufacture or alter a document just to make the address requirement work. If you do not have the normal proof of residency, look for the state's alternative-document list or contact the issuing agency before your appointment.

What if your documents are in a different name?

Bring the paper trail connecting the names.

For example, someone who changed their name after marriage may need their marriage certificate in addition to their identity document. Someone whose name changed through a court order may need the certified court document.

The exact chain depends on the state and how many times the name changed.

The goal is simple: the agency needs to be able to connect the person standing at the counter with the documents being presented.

What if you do not have a passport?

You do not automatically need a passport to get a state ID.

States generally provide multiple acceptable identity-document options. A certified birth certificate is one common example for eligible applicants.

For REAL ID, USAGov lists several possible identity documents, including a U.S. birth certificate, passport and Permanent Resident Card. citeturn0search1

The key is to use the list published by your state rather than assuming one particular document is mandatory nationwide.

One mistake to avoid: buying something that only looks like an ID

When people are struggling to get a government credential, they sometimes run into websites selling novelty cards or documents designed to resemble official identification.

Those are not substitutes for a state-issued ID.

FakeIDs' explanation of what a fake ID is is useful here because it separates the appearance of an identification card from the authority behind a genuine government credential.

And if you are researching identification from an educational or legal perspective, its discussion of how IDs may be examined for authenticity illustrates why a card that looks convincing is not the same thing as a credential issued by a government agency.

For a state ID application, use the official process. There is no legitimate shortcut around the state's identity requirements.

How long does it take to receive the card?

That depends on the state.

Some states issue a temporary document or receipt while the physical card is produced. Others mail the card after the application is approved.

California currently says a new ID card is mailed within three to four weeks, although processing times can change. citeturn0search3

Check your state's instructions for its current processing and delivery timeline.

The easiest way to avoid a second DMV trip

Before you book an appointment, put every requirement in one place.

First-time state ID checklist:
  • ☐ I found my state's official motor vehicle agency.
  • ☐ I confirmed that I am applying for a state ID, not a driver's license.
  • ☐ I decided whether I want a REAL ID.
  • ☐ I have an accepted identity and date-of-birth document.
  • ☐ I have the required Social Security documentation or information.
  • ☐ I have the required proof of state residency.
  • ☐ I have legal name-change documents if my names do not match.
  • ☐ I checked the current fee.
  • ☐ I know whether I need an appointment.
  • ☐ I know whether original or certified documents are required.

That last point matters. For some documents, an ordinary photocopy is not enough. California's REAL ID checklist, for example, specifies original or certified identity documents for qualifying proof. citeturn0search7

Frequently asked questions

How do I get a state ID for the first time?

Apply through your state's motor vehicle or driver's licensing agency, provide the required identity, residency and Social Security documentation, pay the applicable fee and complete the agency's identity and photo requirements.

Do I need a driver's license first?

No. A state ID is a separate identification credential and does not require you to have driving privileges.

What documents do I need for a first state ID?

The exact list varies by state. Common requirements include proof of identity and date of birth, Social Security information and proof of state residency. REAL ID applications generally require specific documents in each category. citeturn0search1

Can I get a REAL ID as my first state ID?

Yes, where your state offers REAL ID-compliant identification cards. The state determines the application process and documents required. citeturn0search1

Can I apply for a state ID online?

It depends on the state and your situation. Some states let you start an application online, but you may still need to visit an office to finish the process.

What happens if I do not have proof of residency?

Check your state's alternative-document rules. Some states accept several types of residency evidence, while others have specific alternatives for people in unusual housing situations.

How much does a first state ID cost?

There is no nationwide fee. States set their own prices, and some offer reduced-fee or no-fee IDs for eligible applicants.
