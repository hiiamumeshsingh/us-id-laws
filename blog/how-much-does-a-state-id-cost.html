How Much Does a State ID Cost? Fees by State Explained

Understand the difference between REAL ID-compliant credentials and standard state-issued identification.

Last reviewed: September 2026

Getting a state ID is usually not expensive. The frustrating part is that there is no single U.S. price.

Each state sets its own fee, and the amount can change depending on whether you are applying for your first ID, renewing an existing card, replacing a lost card or qualifying for a reduced-fee or free credential.

Short answer: There is no nationwide state ID fee. The price depends on the state and the type of ID you are getting. For example, Georgia currently lists $32 for an eight-year Georgia ID card, while California lists $40 for a regular ID card and offers reduced-fee and no-fee options for some applicants. Virginia calculates its standard photo ID fee at $2 per year, with a $10 minimum and $16 maximum.

Why does the cost of a state ID vary?

A state ID is issued by the state, so the state decides what it costs. That means two people applying for essentially the same type of credential can pay very different amounts depending on where they live.

The fee can also depend on the transaction. A first-time application, renewal and replacement are not always priced the same way.

There may also be special categories for seniors, people with disabilities, low-income applicants, veterans or other groups. Some states waive the fee entirely in specific circumstances.

That is why a national "average state ID cost" can be misleading. The number that matters is the fee published by your state's motor vehicle agency for your specific transaction.

How much does a state ID cost in different states?

Here are a few current examples to show how much the pricing structure can differ:

Is a first-time state ID more expensive?

Not necessarily.

Some states charge the same basic fee for an original ID and a renewal. Others use a different fee structure. There can also be separate rules for applicants who qualify for special programs.

The bigger issue with a first-time ID is often not the card fee. It is the paperwork.

You may need to bring documents proving your identity, date of birth, Social Security number and residency. If you are applying for REAL ID, the documentation requirements can be more extensive.

If you are preparing for your first application, our guide to what counts as proof of identity explains the documents states commonly accept.

How much does it cost to replace a lost state ID?

Replacement fees vary by state and by the reason for the replacement.

Some states charge a separate duplicate-card fee. Others use a standard replacement fee or make the replacement free in certain circumstances.

Georgia, for example, lists a replacement duplicate license, permit or ID card fee in its fee schedule, while California includes lost or stolen ID replacement within its regular ID-card fee structure. citeturn1search2turn1search7

Before paying for a replacement, check whether your state distinguishes between a lost card, a stolen card, a damaged card and a card that needs information changed.

If you lost the card and also lost the documents needed to prove your identity, that can become a separate problem. USAGov notes that replacement rules vary and directs people to their state motor vehicle agency for a replacement state ID. citeturn2search2

Do you pay more for a REAL ID?

Not always.

REAL ID is a federal standard for state-issued driver's licenses and identification cards. The state still controls the application process and fees.

USAGov explains that you can choose to make a driver's license or state ID REAL ID-compliant when applying or renewing, but the documents and process are determined by your state. citeturn2search4

That means you should not assume that "REAL ID" automatically means "extra fee." In some states, the cost may be built into a normal transaction. In others, upgrading or replacing a credential can involve a different charge.

If you want to understand the difference before you pay, see our guide to REAL ID vs. a standard driver's license.

Are there free or reduced-fee state IDs?

Yes. Some states have programs that reduce or eliminate the normal ID fee for people who meet specific requirements.

California is a clear example. Its current fee schedule lists a regular ID at $40, a reduced-fee ID at $11 and no fee for a senior citizen ID card. citeturn1search7

Georgia also lists certain no-fee and $5 ID categories, including specific voter, veteran, disability and qualifying assistance programs. citeturn1search0turn1search6

The eligibility rules matter. A reduced-fee program is not necessarily available simply because an applicant wants a cheaper card.

Do seniors pay less for a state ID?

In some states, yes.

California, for example, currently lists a no-fee senior citizen ID for applicants age 62 or older. citeturn1search7

But this is not a nationwide rule. Another state may use a normal fee, a different age threshold or a separate senior credential.

If you are applying for a parent or older family member, check the state's fee schedule instead of assuming a senior discount exists.

Do children pay less for a state ID?

Sometimes. Again, the answer depends on the state.

Some states issue IDs to children and minors under separate fee schedules or validity periods. Others may charge the same basic fee but use a different expiration period.

Virginia, for example, has different validity rules for child ID cards while its standard photo ID fee is calculated by years of validity. citeturn1search1turn1search5

The fee is only one part of the calculation. Before applying for a minor, check the state's age requirements, parent or guardian rules, documents and validity period.

Can a state ID cost more if you change your name?

It can, depending on what transaction the state requires.

A name change may be handled as an amendment, replacement or full reissuance. The state may also require certified documentation connecting the old name to the new one.

USAGov recommends updating your name with the Social Security Administration and your state motor vehicle office after a legal name change. Certified marriage or divorce documents and court orders may be used as supporting evidence. citeturn2search5

If your documents show different names, fix the paperwork before the appointment when possible. A fee is annoying. A rejected application and second trip to the DMV is worse.

Are there other costs besides the ID fee?

Yes. The card fee may not be the only expense.

  • Document replacement: You may need a certified birth certificate or another identity document before you can apply.
  • Transportation: Some applications require an in-person DMV visit.
  • Copies or certified records: A state may require certified documents rather than ordinary photocopies.
  • Name-change records: Marriage certificates, divorce decrees or court orders may be needed when names do not match.
  • Additional services: Some states charge separate fees for special transactions or credentials.

So when budgeting for a state ID, think beyond the price printed next to "ID card." The supporting documents can sometimes cost more than the card itself.

What if you cannot afford the state ID fee?

Do not assume the normal fee is your only option.

Check whether your state offers a reduced-fee, no-fee or special-purpose ID program. Eligibility may be based on age, income, disability, veteran status, voter registration or participation in a qualifying assistance program.

For example, Georgia lists no-fee and $5 ID categories for certain applicants, while California lists both a reduced-fee ID and a no-fee senior ID. citeturn1search0turn1search7

If you are unsure which category applies to you, contact the state's motor vehicle agency before paying the standard fee.

Does a state ID fee cover REAL ID automatically?

No. Paying for an ID does not by itself tell you whether the credential is REAL ID-compliant.

REAL ID is about the credential and the issuance requirements, not simply the amount you pay. USAGov says a REAL ID-compliant state ID generally requires proof of identity, Social Security number and state residency, with the exact process controlled by the state. citeturn2search4

If you are applying specifically because you want a REAL ID for federal purposes, tell the DMV that when you apply and bring the documents listed by your state.

State ID fees are not the same as the value of the ID

It is easy to look at a $10, $20, $40 or higher fee and think you are simply paying for a plastic card.

You are really paying for a credential issued through a government identity system. The state verifies the underlying information, creates the record and issues the credential under its own rules.

That distinction matters when comparing a genuine state-issued ID with documents that merely claim to represent government identification. As FakeIDs explains in its guide to what makes an ID "fake", a legitimate ID is backed by the issuing authority and its verification process. A card that simply looks official does not have that same status.

For a related look at the legal distinction between counterfeit identification and clearly fictional props or collectibles, FakeIDs' guide to collectible ID legal boundaries explains why the design and intended use matter.

How to find your state's exact ID fee

Do not rely on a national estimate if you are about to visit the DMV. Use this process instead:

  1. Find your state's official motor vehicle agency through USAGov's state motor vehicle directory.
  2. Look for the agency's fee schedule or identification-card page.
  3. Choose the transaction that actually applies: original, renewal, replacement, amendment or REAL ID.
  4. Check whether you qualify for a reduced-fee or no-fee program.
  5. Check the accepted payment methods before visiting.
  6. Confirm whether additional document or service fees apply.

This takes a few minutes and can save you from arriving at the DMV with the wrong amount or the wrong documents.

Before you go: Check the official fee schedule on the same day you plan your appointment if possible. State fees can change, and third-party websites often keep old prices online long after a DMV updates its schedule.

State ID cost checklist

  • ☐ I know whether this is an original, renewal or replacement application.

  • ☐ I have the required identity and residency documents.
  • ☐ My name is consistent across my documents, or I have legal proof of the name change.
  • ☐ I know which payment methods the DMV accepts.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a state ID usually cost?

There is no standard national price. Current examples include $40 for a regular California ID, $32 for an eight-year Georgia ID and $10 to $16 for a standard Virginia photo ID depending on validity. New York's current guide lists $13 for a non-driver ID, subject to its issuance rules. citeturn1search7turn1search0turn1search1turn1search48

Is a REAL ID more expensive than a regular state ID?

Not necessarily. States set their own fees and processes. A REAL ID upgrade may have a different charge in some situations, while in others the normal transaction covers it.

Can I get a free state ID?

Possibly. Some states have no-fee programs for specific groups, such as certain seniors, veterans, voters or people who meet other eligibility requirements.

Does replacing a lost ID cost the same as getting a new one?

Not always. Some states use a separate replacement fee, while others include replacement in a broader fee category. Check your state's current schedule.

Do I need to pay for an ID before I know if my documents are accepted?

The process varies. In many cases the application and fee are part of the same transaction, so it is worth checking your state's document requirements before you go.

Where can I check the latest state ID fee?

Start with your state's official motor vehicle agency. USAGov maintains a directory that links to state motor vehicle services. citeturn2search0
