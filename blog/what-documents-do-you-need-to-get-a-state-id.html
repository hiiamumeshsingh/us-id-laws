What Documents Do You Need to Get a State ID?

A practical guide to the documents states commonly ask for when you apply for a state identification card.

Last reviewed: September 2026

The frustrating part of getting a state ID is rarely the card itself. It is showing up at the DMV with the wrong document and finding out that your proof of address is too old, your name does not match, or the copy of your birth certificate is not acceptable.

The good news is that the paperwork is easier to understand once you separate it into a few basic questions: Who are you? Where do you live? What is your Social Security status? And has your legal name changed?

Short answer: Most state ID applications require some combination of proof of identity, date of birth, state residency and Social Security number or status. Some applicants also need proof of lawful status or citizenship and documents connecting a former legal name to the current one. The exact list is set by each state.

The four document categories you should expect

State agencies use different checklists, but the same document categories appear again and again.

What you need to proveCommon examplesWhy it matters
Identity and date of birthU.S. passport, certified birth certificate, permanent resident cardEstablishes who you are and when you were born
State residencyLease, utility bill, bank statement, insurance documentShows that you live in the state
Social Security numberSocial Security card, W-2, pay stub or other accepted recordConnects the application to your Social Security record where required
Name changeMarriage certificate, divorce decree, court orderConnects your current legal name to older documents

That is the framework. Your state's checklist may ask for more, fewer or different documents within each category.

1. Proof of identity and date of birth

This is usually the first thing the agency needs to establish. The document has to show that you are the person applying and, in many cases, your date of birth.

Depending on the state and type of ID, commonly accepted documents can include:

  • A valid U.S. passport or passport card
  • An original or certified U.S. birth certificate
  • A Certificate of Naturalization or Certificate of Citizenship
  • A Permanent Resident Card
  • Certain foreign passports and immigration documents
  • Other state-approved identity documents

For example, California's REAL ID checklist accepts a valid U.S. passport, an original or certified birth certificate, a Permanent Resident Card and certain other immigration documents as proof of identity. urlCalifornia DMV REAL ID checklisthttps://www.dmv.ca.gov/portal/driver-licenses-identification-cards/real-id/real-id-checklist/

The important detail is not every document that proves something about you is automatically proof of identity. A bank statement may prove your address, for example, but that does not make it a substitute for the identity document your state requires.

If you want to understand the distinction in more detail, see our guide to what counts as proof of identity.

2. Proof of state residency

States also need to establish that you actually live in the state where you are applying.

This is where documents such as these commonly come in:

  • Lease or rental agreement
  • Utility or mobile phone bill
  • Bank or credit union statement
  • Insurance document
  • Mortgage statement or property record
  • Government correspondence
  • Vehicle registration
  • School or employment records, where accepted

The rules can be surprisingly specific. California's REAL ID process, for example, requires two different residency documents showing the applicant's name and California mailing address. Its checklist also explains how some applicants can use documents in another person's name when they provide an acceptable document connecting the relationship. urlCalifornia DMV residency requirementshttps://www.dmv.ca.gov/portal/driver-licenses-identification-cards/real-id/real-id-checklist/

New York uses its own document system and distinguishes between the residency proof needed for a standard credential and the additional proof required for REAL ID or Enhanced credentials. urlNew York DMV document guidehttps://dmv.ny.gov/forms/id44.pdf

So do not assume that one utility bill is enough just because a friend in another state used one.

3. Proof of your Social Security number

Many states ask for your Social Security number as part of the application. That does not always mean you must bring your physical Social Security card.

Depending on the state's rules, acceptable evidence may include a Social Security card, W-2, pay stub or another official document showing the required information.

For REAL ID applications, USAGov says states generally require proof of identity, Social Security number and state residency. It gives a Social Security card, W-2 or pay stub as examples of Social Security documentation. urlUSAGov REAL ID requirementshttps://www.usa.gov/real-id

This is one reason it is worth reading the actual checklist before your appointment. The agency may accept several forms of evidence, but the exact options are not identical everywhere.

4. Proof of citizenship or lawful status

This category can be confusing because it overlaps with identity documents but is not exactly the same thing.

A passport or birth certificate can establish identity and citizenship at the same time. Other applicants may use immigration documents that establish lawful status.

REAL ID applications generally involve additional federal documentation standards. USAGov lists a U.S. birth certificate, U.S. passport and Permanent Resident Card among examples of identity documents used for REAL ID applications. urlUSAGov REAL ID requirementshttps://www.usa.gov/real-id

The exact documents accepted for a non-REAL-ID state card depend on the state. That distinction matters, especially if you are applying for a standard card rather than a REAL ID.

5. What if your name has changed?

This is one of the most common reasons a document packet falls apart.

Imagine your birth certificate says one name, your Social Security record has another and your current application uses a third because you recently married. The DMV needs a paper trail connecting those names.

Depending on the situation, that may mean bringing a certified marriage certificate, divorce record or court order showing the legal name change.

California's REAL ID checklist specifically notes that additional name-change documentation is required when the name on the identity document differs from the applicant's current name. urlCalifornia DMV REAL ID checklisthttps://www.dmv.ca.gov/portal/driver-licenses-identification-cards/real-id/real-id-checklist/

It is much easier to bring that document with you than to discover at the counter that your application cannot be completed without it.

What if the bill or lease is not in your name?

This happens all the time with people who live with parents, spouses, roommates or other household members.

Some states have specific ways to handle it. California, for example, allows certain applicants to use a document that traces their relationship to the person named on the residency document. For minors, a birth certificate can establish the relationship to a parent whose name appears on the residency document. urlCalifornia DMV REAL ID checklisthttps://www.dmv.ca.gov/portal/driver-licenses-identification-cards/real-id/real-id-checklist/

Other states use different procedures. The important point is that "the bill is not in my name" does not automatically mean you cannot prove residency. It means you need to check your state's accepted alternatives.

Do documents have to be originals?

Sometimes.

Some agencies require an original or certified copy for identity documents, while other documents may be accepted as printed copies or electronic statements. The rules can also differ depending on the type of credential.

For example, California's REAL ID checklist specifies an original or certified copy for certain identity documents and has separate rules for residency evidence. New York's current document guide also specifies particular formats for residence proofs and other documents. urlCalifornia DMV REAL ID checklisthttps://www.dmv.ca.gov/portal/driver-licenses-identification-cards/real-id/real-id-checklist/ urlNew York DMV document guidehttps://dmv.ny.gov/forms/id44.pdf

Do not rely on a phone photo of a document unless the agency explicitly says it accepts one.

What documents do you need for a REAL ID state ID?

The REAL ID version of a state ID generally requires a more defined documentation package than a basic state credential.

USAGov says that, in most cases, the state licensing agency will require proof of identity, Social Security number and residency for a REAL ID. urlUSAGov REAL ID requirementshttps://www.usa.gov/real-id

California provides a clear example of how that can look in practice:

  • One accepted proof of identity
  • Two different proofs of state residency
  • Social Security number information
  • Name-change documents when applicable

That is a California example, not a nationwide checklist. Your state's requirements may differ.

What if you have no photo ID right now?

Not having a current driver's license or state ID does not necessarily prevent you from applying for one.

That is exactly why states maintain first-time identification processes. The agency may use a passport, birth certificate, immigration document or another accepted record to establish your identity.

The key is to start with the state's official list of first-time applicants rather than assuming that you need an existing photo ID to obtain your first state ID.

If you are replacing a lost credential rather than applying for the first time, the process may be different. USAGov directs people seeking replacement state IDs to their state motor vehicle agency because replacement rules vary. urlUSAGov replacement ID guidancehttps://www.usa.gov/replace-vital-documents

Why one state's checklist cannot be used for another state

This is probably the biggest mistake people make when researching ID requirements online.

A friend tells you they used a lease and a birth certificate. A website says you need two utility bills. Someone else says a passport is enough.

All three statements can be accurate in the right state and still be wrong for your application.

USAGov maintains a state-by-state directory for motor vehicle services because each state controls its own licensing and identification procedures. urlUSAGov state motor vehicle serviceshttps://www.usa.gov/state-motor-vehicle-services

That is the source you should use to find your state's agency. From there, look specifically for the document checklist for the credential you are applying for.

Do fake or novelty IDs count as supporting documents?

No. A document that merely looks like a government credential is not a substitute for a document accepted by the state issuing agency.

That distinction matters because the word ID is used loosely online. A state-issued identification card is backed by an official issuance process. A counterfeit or altered document is not.

For a useful explanation of that distinction, FakeIDs.com's guide to what a fake ID means explains why the important issue is the government authority a document claims to represent.

There is also a difference between a counterfeit document and a clearly fictional collectible or prop. FakeIDs.com's discussion of the legal boundaries of collectible IDs looks at that distinction from an educational perspective.

Neither category replaces the official documents required for a state ID application.

Your state ID document checklist

Before leaving for the DMV, run through this list:

  • ☐ I checked the official requirements for my state.
  • ☐ I know whether I am applying for a standard ID or REAL ID.
  • ☐ I have an accepted identity and date-of-birth document.
  • ☐ I have the required proof of state residency.
  • ☐ I have the required Social Security documentation or know what alternative the state accepts.
  • ☐ My current legal name matches my documents, or I have the required name-change records.
  • ☐ I know whether my documents must be original, certified or printed.
  • ☐ I checked the current fee and appointment rules.
The safest rule: Do not build your document packet from a generic national checklist. Start with your state's official motor vehicle agency and follow the requirements for the exact ID card you are applying for.

Frequently asked questions

What is usually the most important document for getting a state ID?

There is no single document that works everywhere. A passport or certified birth certificate is commonly used to establish identity and date of birth, but each state decides which documents it accepts.

Can I use a birth certificate as proof of identity?

In many state ID and REAL ID processes, an original or certified birth certificate is an accepted identity document. The state may also require separate proof of residency and other information.

Can a utility bill prove my identity?

Usually, a utility bill is used as proof of residency rather than primary proof of identity. The exact treatment depends on the state.

Do I need my Social Security card to get a state ID?

Not necessarily. Some states accept other documents showing your Social Security number or have alternative procedures. Check your state's current checklist.

What if my documents have different names?

You may need a certified marriage record, divorce document, court order or another accepted record connecting your previous name to your current legal name.

Can I get a REAL ID state ID without a driver's license?

Yes. REAL ID can apply to state identification cards as well as driver's licenses, although the exact application process and documents are controlled by the state.

How many proof-of-residency documents do I need?

It depends on the state and credential. Some applications require one document while REAL ID applications may require two or more. Check the official state checklist.

Where can I find my state's official ID document list?

USAGov's state motor vehicle services directory is a useful starting point for finding the correct state agency. Once there, use the agency's identification-card or non-driver-ID requirements.

Source-first reminder: ID requirements, accepted documents, fees and application procedures can change. US ID Laws explains the system, but the issuing state agency is the final source to verify before you apply.
