What Counts as Proof of Identity?

What documents can prove who you are, what each document actually proves and what to check before you submit one.

Last reviewed: September 2026

Quick answer: There is no single U.S.-wide list of documents that works for every situation. The agency, state, credential and purpose matter. A driver's license, state ID or U.S. passport is commonly used as identity evidence, but some processes accept other documents or require additional proof.

What does “proof of identity” mean?

Proof of identity is a document an agency or organization uses to establish that you are the person named on an application or record. Depending on the process, it may need to show your legal name, date of birth, photograph or other identifying information.

The important part is knowing what the document is being used to prove. Identity, citizenship, residency and Social Security information are not automatically the same requirement. One document can sometimes satisfy more than one requirement, but you should not assume that it will.

What documents are commonly accepted?

The exact list depends on the agency and transaction. For example, the Social Security Administration lists a U.S. driver's license, state-issued non-driver identification card and U.S. passport as primary examples of identity evidence. It also describes certain school, employee, health insurance and military documents as possible alternatives in specific circumstances. See SSA's identity-document guidance.

Is a birth certificate proof of identity?

It can be an important identity-related document, but it is not universally accepted as a standalone identity credential. A birth certificate primarily documents birth information and may also be used to establish citizenship. A separate identity document may still be required.

SSA's documentation guidance illustrates this distinction by treating evidence of age, citizenship and identity as separate categories. Its identity examples include a driver's license, state-issued non-driver ID or U.S. passport. See SSA Form SS-5 guidance.

Is a Social Security card proof of identity?

Generally, no. A Social Security card is used to show your Social Security number, not as a general-purpose photo ID. SSA specifically states that the Social Security card is not an identification document.

That distinction matters because an application may ask for both identity evidence and Social Security information. A Social Security card may satisfy one part of the request without satisfying the identity requirement.

Proof of identity vs. proof of residency

These two requirements are easy to mix up, but they answer different questions:

  • Proof of identity: Who are you?
  • Proof of residency: Where do you live?
  • Proof of citizenship or lawful status: What citizenship or immigration status applies to you?
  • Social Security information: What SSN or Social Security record can be verified?

A driver's license may establish identity, while a lease, utility bill or other accepted address document may be used for residency. The exact combination depends on the agency.

How REAL ID changes the document question

REAL ID is a federal standard for certain state-issued driver's licenses and identification cards. States still issue the credentials, so the application process and document checklist are state-specific.

The federal REAL ID framework includes requirements involving identity, date of birth, Social Security information, address and lawful status. That means simply having an existing government ID does not necessarily tell you everything you need for a REAL ID application. See the DHS REAL ID framework.

What if the names on your documents do not match?

A name mismatch can create an additional documentation requirement. This can happen after marriage, divorce or another legal name change.

The agency may ask for a document that connects the old and new names, such as a marriage certificate, divorce decree or court order. TSA's enrollment guidance provides one federal example where name-linking documentation can be required. See TSA's current guidance.

What if you do not have a government photo ID?

Do not assume that you have no options. Some agencies have alternative-document or exception procedures, but those alternatives can be narrow and program-specific.

SSA, for example, describes circumstances where school, employee, health insurance or military documents may be accepted when a primary identity document is unavailable. See SSA's document list.

The safest approach is to check the official list for the exact agency and transaction rather than relying on a generic checklist.

What about fake, altered or novelty IDs?

A document that looks like an identification card is not automatically a valid government credential. A novelty or replica card should not be presented as a genuine government ID.

For background on the term “fake ID” and the distinction between fake identification and novelty cards, see What Is a Fake ID? Definition, Types, and Legal Facts on FakeIDs.com.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming a birth certificate alone proves identity everywhere.
  • Treating a Social Security card as a general-purpose photo ID.
  • Using an expired document when the agency requires current evidence.
  • Ignoring a legal-name mismatch between documents.
  • Using a generic checklist instead of the current official agency list.
  • Confusing proof of identity with proof of residency or citizenship.

Frequently asked questions

Is a birth certificate proof of identity?

It can be important identity-related evidence, but it is not universally accepted as standalone identity proof. Some processes require a separate identity document.

Is a Social Security card proof of identity?

Generally, no. SSA specifically says its Social Security card is not an identification document.

Is a driver's license proof of identity?

Often, yes. A current state-issued driver's license is commonly used as identity evidence, but the receiving agency's rules control.

Can one document prove identity and citizenship?

Sometimes. A U.S. passport is one example, but you should check the specific agency's requirements.

What should I do if my documents have different names?

Check whether the agency requires a name-linking document such as a marriage certificate, divorce decree or court order.

Do identification requirements differ by state?

Yes. State agencies set many requirements for state-issued credentials, so document lists can differ between states and transactions.

Where to verify the requirement

The best source is the government agency handling your transaction. For federal identity documentation, SSA publishes its own evidence requirements. For REAL ID, DHS provides the federal framework while state agencies provide the state-specific process.
