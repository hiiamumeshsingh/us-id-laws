How to Replace a Lost or Stolen State ID

Understand the difference between REAL ID-compliant credentials and standard state-issued identification.

Last reviewed: September 2026

Losing your state ID is one of those small problems that can suddenly make everything else harder.

You need identification to do something else, but the identification is the thing you just lost.

The good news is that replacing a state ID is usually much simpler than getting one for the first time. The important part is figuring out how your state handles replacement and whether you need to do anything differently because the card was stolen rather than simply misplaced.

Quick answer: To replace a lost, stolen or damaged state ID, contact the state agency that issued it and follow its replacement process. Depending on the state, you may be able to request a replacement online, by mail or in person. You may need to verify your identity, pay a replacement fee and provide additional documents if the agency cannot verify your record.

What should you do first?

If you cannot find your ID, do not immediately assume it was stolen.

Retrace your steps, check the places you normally keep it and make sure someone did not turn it in. If it is clearly gone, move on to the replacement process rather than waiting weeks hoping it will turn up.

If you believe it was stolen, treat that a little differently. Someone else may have your personal information, and the card itself may be usable as part of an attempt to impersonate you.

Your state's replacement instructions are the starting point. USAGov says replacement rules for state ID cards vary and directs people to their state motor vehicle agency. citeturn0search0

Lost and stolen are not always the same thing

From the DMV's point of view, both situations can lead to the same basic replacement application. But what you do around the replacement can be different.

Situation What to do
Lost Request a replacement through your state agency and follow its identity-verification process.
Stolen Request a replacement and consider reporting the theft if appropriate, especially if other personal documents were taken.
Damaged Check whether the state requires you to surrender or return the damaged card.
Name or address changed You may need an update transaction rather than a simple duplicate-card request.

The exact rules are state-specific. California, for example, has a replacement process for lost, stolen or damaged ID cards and requires an office visit for ID-card replacement. citeturn0search4

Can you replace a state ID online?

Sometimes.

This is one of those questions where a search result from another state can be more confusing than helpful.

Some states allow online replacement if your record can be verified and you meet certain conditions. Others require an in-person visit for an ID card replacement.

California is an example of the second approach: its current instructions say an ID-card replacement is completed through an office visit, while some driver's-license replacement transactions can be handled online. citeturn0search4

So if you find a page saying "replace your ID online," make sure it is actually talking about your state's identification card, not a driver's license, vehicle registration or another document.

What documents do you need to replace a lost ID?

If your state can locate and verify your existing record, the replacement may require very little paperwork.

But if the agency cannot verify your identity, you may have to provide additional documents.

USAGov notes that many government ID replacement processes require an official document such as a birth certificate to establish identity or citizenship. citeturn0search0

The documents can vary depending on the state and the reason for replacement. You might be asked for:

  • Your full name and date of birth
  • Your existing ID number, if you know it
  • Social Security information
  • Proof of identity
  • Proof of residency
  • Immigration or lawful-presence documents, where applicable
  • Legal name-change documents, if your records have changed

That does not mean you will need every item on that list. It means you should check the state's replacement page before assuming the old card is enough to identify you.

What if you lost all your identification?

This is where the process can become frustrating.

Suppose your wallet disappeared and it contained your state ID, Social Security card, debit cards and insurance card. Now you are being asked for ID to replace the ID you no longer have.

Do not make up a substitute document. Instead, work through the state's accepted identity-document list.

USAGov notes that states may require official documents such as a birth certificate for replacement identification. citeturn0search0

If your birth certificate is also missing, that becomes a separate replacement process through the vital records office in the state or territory where the birth occurred.

In other words, you may have to rebuild your document trail one piece at a time.

What if the ID was stolen?

If your wallet or purse was stolen, think beyond the ID card.

The bigger problem may be everything that was with it.

If bank cards, checks, Social Security documents, passports or other sensitive records were stolen at the same time, take the appropriate steps with the organizations that issued them.

For the state ID itself, follow the issuing state's replacement procedure. USAGov directs people to the state motor vehicle agency for replacement state IDs. citeturn0search0

If you believe the theft involved identity theft rather than simply a stolen wallet, you may also want to review the federal government's identity-theft guidance and monitor accounts connected to the missing documents.

Do you need to report a stolen state ID to the police?

There is no single nationwide rule requiring a police report for every stolen state ID.

Your state's DMV may or may not ask for one. A police report can still be useful when a wallet containing several documents was stolen, particularly if you later need a record of what happened.

Do not assume that filing a police report automatically replaces the DMV's replacement process. You still need to follow the state's procedure for getting a new card.

Can someone use your lost state ID?

A state ID contains personal information, so a lost or stolen card should not be treated as harmless.

The risk depends on what else the person has and what they try to do with the information.

A card by itself does not automatically give someone access to your bank account or government benefits. But it can expose your name, date of birth, address and other information that could be combined with data from another source.

If your wallet was stolen, look at the incident as a personal-information exposure, not just a missing piece of plastic.

Will the old ID still work after you order a replacement?

Once a replacement is issued, you should not assume that the old card remains valid.

California's published guidance says that once a replacement driver's license is issued, the previous license is no longer valid. citeturn0search24

For state ID cards, follow the specific instructions from your issuing agency. If the original card turns up after the replacement arrives, do not start carrying both as though they are two active credentials.

If the agency tells you to destroy or surrender the old card, follow that instruction.

What if you find the old ID after ordering a replacement?

Finding the card can be surprisingly awkward because the replacement process may already have changed the status of the old credential.

Do not assume that because the card is physically in your hand it is still valid.

Check the state's instructions or contact the issuing agency. If a replacement has already been issued, the agency can tell you what to do with the old card.

What does replacing a REAL ID involve?

If your lost card was REAL ID-compliant, replacing it does not necessarily mean you have to start the entire REAL ID process from zero.

The agency already has a record of the credential, but the replacement process depends on the state.

California, for example, provides a replacement process for its ID cards and separately maintains REAL ID requirements. citeturn0search4turn0search15

If your circumstances have changed since the original REAL ID was issued — such as a name or immigration-status change — the agency may require updated documents.

What if your name has changed since the old ID was issued?

Do not treat this as a simple lost-card replacement.

If the old card has your former name and you now use a legally changed name, you may need an update transaction and supporting documentation.

California's ID guidance says the current true full name must appear on the identity document and directs applicants with name changes to the appropriate update process. citeturn0search16

That can mean bringing a marriage certificate, divorce record or court order, depending on the circumstances and the state's rules.

What if you moved since your old ID was issued?

Again, this may be more than a replacement.

If your state requires your current address on the card, you may need to update your address as part of the replacement transaction.

That can mean bringing proof of residency, especially if the state's system cannot verify the new address electronically.

If you are not sure what counts, our guide to proof of residency for a state ID covers the common documents and the problems people run into when the bill or lease is not in their name.

What if you are a non-U.S. citizen and lose your state ID?

The replacement process can involve one extra layer: your immigration or lawful-presence documentation.

If your state issued a credential based on temporary legal status, the agency may need to verify that status again. That can matter if your immigration document has expired, your status has changed or the original ID was tied to a particular authorized period.

If you are a noncitizen, keep your current immigration documents available when checking the replacement requirements.

Our guide on state IDs for non-U.S. citizens explains the relationship between state identification, immigration documents and REAL ID in more detail.

How much does it cost to replace a state ID?

There is no national replacement fee.

States set their own fees, and the price can depend on the type of credential and transaction.

California, for example, publishes a replacement process and applicable licensing fee, while some states have separate replacement fees or reduced-fee programs. citeturn0search4turn0search16

Some applicants may qualify for special fee programs. California currently lists reduced-fee and no-fee ID categories for qualifying applicants. citeturn0search16

For a broader look at how state ID pricing works, see How Much Does a State ID Cost?

What if you need identification before the replacement arrives?

This is one of the practical annoyances of losing an ID.

Some states provide a temporary document, receipt or other evidence after the replacement application. Others may simply mail the new card later.

Do not assume a DMV receipt will be accepted everywhere as a substitute for a photo ID. A business, employer, airline or government agency may have its own identification requirements.

If you urgently need identification for a particular purpose, ask that organization what credentials it accepts rather than assuming your replacement receipt will work.

Do not buy a replacement from an unofficial website

A state ID is issued by the state. The replacement process should therefore begin with the state's official agency.

You may see websites offering to "replace" or "recreate" an ID without going through the DMV. That is not the same thing as getting a replacement government credential.

FakeIDs' explanation of what makes an ID fake is useful for understanding the basic distinction: the legal status of an identification card comes from the issuing authority, not merely from its appearance.

And if you are interested in the separate problem of genuine government IDs being questioned because they look unfamiliar, this discussion of legitimate driver's licenses being mistaken for fake IDs shows why appearance and authenticity are two different questions.

If your real ID is lost, the legitimate replacement route is boring for a reason: the state has to connect the new card to your existing identity record.

A simple replacement process

In most cases, the process looks roughly like this:

  1. Confirm the card is actually missing.
  2. Find the official state replacement page.
  3. Check whether your ID can be replaced online.
  4. Gather any required identity documents.
  5. Update your address or name if those details have changed.
  6. Pay the replacement fee, if one applies.
  7. Complete any required photo, fingerprint or in-person verification.
  8. Wait for the replacement card or temporary credential.

California's current ID replacement process, for example, requires an application and office visit for an ID-card replacement, followed by photo validation and a thumbprint scan, with the new card mailed afterward. citeturn0search4

Another state may let you do most or all of the process online. That is why the state-specific page matters.

What if you cannot remember your ID number?

That is usually not a reason to panic.

The state already has your record. Your ID number can be useful, but it is not necessarily the only way the agency can locate you.

You may be able to verify your identity using your name, date of birth, Social Security information or other details already associated with your record.

If the online replacement system requires information you do not have, use the agency's alternate process rather than guessing.

Replacement checklist

Before you submit the replacement request:
  • ☐ I checked my state's official DMV or motor vehicle agency.
  • ☐ I know whether the card is lost, stolen or damaged.
  • ☐ I checked whether online replacement is available for an ID card.
  • ☐ I know the replacement fee.
  • ☐ I have any identity documents the agency may require.
  • ☐ I checked whether my address needs to be updated.
  • ☐ I checked whether my name needs to be updated.
  • ☐ If I am a noncitizen, I have my current immigration documents available.
  • ☐ I know what to do if the old card turns up later.

Frequently asked questions

How do I replace a lost state ID?

Contact the state agency that issued the card and follow its replacement process. Depending on the state, replacement may be available online, by mail or in person. citeturn0search0

Can I replace my state ID online?

Some states allow it, but not all. The availability can also depend on whether you are replacing an ID card or a driver's license.

What if my state ID was stolen?

Follow the state's replacement procedure and consider reporting the theft if appropriate, especially if other sensitive documents were stolen with it.

Do I need my birth certificate to replace a lost ID?

Not always. If the state can verify your existing record, you may not need it. If additional identity verification is required, the state may ask for an official identity document such as a birth certificate. citeturn0search0

How much does it cost to replace a state ID?

There is no nationwide fee. The state sets the replacement price and may offer reduced-fee or no-fee options for eligible applicants.

What happens if I find my old ID after getting a replacement?

Do not assume it is still valid. Check your state's instructions because the old credential may no longer be valid after a replacement is issued.

Can I replace a REAL ID if I lose it?

Yes, where the state offers REAL ID credentials. The replacement process is handled by the state agency that issued the card, and updated documentation may be needed if your circumstances have changed.
