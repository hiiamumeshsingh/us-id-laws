RESEARCH STANDARDS

Where Our ID Information Comes From

We prioritize government agencies, laws and other authoritative sources when researching U.S. identification requirements.

Last reviewed: September 25, 2026

Identification rules vary by state and can change over time. Our goal is to trace important claims back to the authority responsible for the requirement.

Our source hierarchy

When researching an identification requirement, we generally start with the source that has direct authority over the rule. A state driver's licensing agency is more useful for a state ID application requirement than a general article about IDs, for example.

01

State agencies

DMVs, departments of motor vehicles, departments of public safety and other state identification authorities are our primary sources for state-issued credentials.

02

Election authorities

State and local election offices are used for voter registration, voter ID, polling and election-document requirements.

03

Federal agencies

Federal agencies and official federal resources are used for matters such as REAL ID, immigration documentation, Social Security and federal identification requirements.

State driver's license and state ID requirements

State agencies are the starting point for information about driver's licenses and non-driver identification cards. Depending on the state, the responsible agency may be called a DMV, Department of Driver Services, Department of Public Safety or another name.

We use these agencies to research requirements such as acceptable identity documents, proof of residency, Social Security documentation, fees, renewals, replacements and application procedures.

Because requirements are state-specific, we do not treat a general national checklist as a substitute for the state's own requirements.

REAL ID and federal identification requirements

For REAL ID topics, we use official federal sources alongside the relevant state licensing agency. USAGov's REAL ID guidance explains the federal framework and directs applicants to their state's licensing agency for the actual application process. citeturn0search1

Federal materials from agencies such as the Department of Homeland Security and Transportation Security Administration can also be relevant when a topic involves federal acceptance of identification or REAL ID implementation.

Voter ID and election information

Voter identification rules are especially state-specific. We rely on state and local election authorities for current requirements and use federal resources for broader explanations of how election rules work.

For a national starting point, USAGov's voter ID guidance explains that each state sets its own voter ID rules and provides links and direction for checking state requirements. citeturn0search0

We may also use the U.S. Election Assistance Commission and official state election resources when researching election-related identification questions.

Social Security and identity documentation

Some identification questions involve Social Security numbers or evidence of identity. For those topics, we use official Social Security Administration material where applicable.

For example, the SSA publishes its own rules and guidance about acceptable evidence of identity and documentation for Social Security services. See the SSA priority list for acceptable evidence of identity. citeturn0search8

Immigration and lawful-status documentation

When an article involves immigration documents, lawful status or verification of federal immigration records, we use official sources from agencies such as U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services and the Department of Homeland Security.

These sources are particularly important for topics where a state agency verifies information against federal records. We do not treat commercial immigration websites or user-generated discussions as substitutes for the underlying federal source.

Statutes, regulations and legal sources

Some questions cannot be answered fully from an agency webpage. When a requirement depends on a statute, regulation or formal legal rule, we may consult the underlying legal source as well.

Where the wording of a law matters, our goal is to distinguish the legal rule from an agency's plain-language explanation of that rule.

How we use secondary sources

Secondary sources can be useful for context, historical background or identifying an issue that needs further research. They are not automatically treated as authoritative.

When a secondary source makes a claim about a current identification requirement, we try to trace that claim back to the responsible government agency, statute, regulation or other primary authority before relying on it.

Sources we do not treat as authoritative

Search results and snippets can help locate information, but they are not themselves the source of a legal or administrative requirement. We also do not treat anonymous forum posts, social media comments, unverified blogs or commercial claims as proof of a current government requirement.

User experiences can sometimes reveal a useful question to investigate, but they are not a replacement for the official rule.

How sources appear on US ID Laws

We aim to link important sources close to the claim or section they support rather than placing every reference in a disconnected list. This gives readers a practical path to verify the information themselves.

For state-specific topics, the relevant state agency should generally be the final check before someone applies for an ID, renews a credential, replaces a document or relies on a requirement for an important transaction.

Keeping sources current

Identification requirements can change because of new legislation, agency policy, administrative updates or implementation deadlines. We review pages when material changes are identified and update source links when the underlying authority changes.

If you notice that an official source has changed or that a page contains outdated information, please contact us with the relevant government source so we can review it.
