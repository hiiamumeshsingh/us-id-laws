Lost or Stolen ID: What to Do Next

A practical guide to reporting a lost or stolen driver's license, state ID or other government-issued identification, requesting a replacement and reducing the risk of further misuse.

First priority
Replace the credential and protect your information.

The right steps depend on which ID was lost, which agency issued it and whether you believe it was stolen or misused.

Educational reference: Replacement procedures, fees, identity checks and reporting requirements vary by state and credential. Use this guide as a starting point, then confirm the current procedure with the agency that issued your ID.

What should you do if you lose your ID?

If your driver's license, state ID or another government-issued credential is missing, start by determining whether it is simply misplaced or whether it may have been stolen. If you cannot locate it, contact the issuing agency and check its replacement procedure.

If the credential was stolen, or if it was lost together with other sensitive documents, you should also consider whether your personal information could be misused. A replacement card solves the immediate credential problem, but it may not address every identity or security concern.

Quick checklist:
  • Check whether the ID can still be located safely
  • Contact the issuing agency if it is missing
  • Follow the official replacement process
  • Ask whether the old credential is cancelled or invalidated
  • Review whether other documents or personal information were lost
  • Take additional identity-protection steps if theft or misuse is suspected

Lost vs. stolen ID

The practical replacement process can be similar, but the circumstances matter. A misplaced ID may simply require a replacement. A stolen ID can raise additional concerns about unauthorized use or exposure of personal information.

Lost ID

If you cannot find the credential, check the issuing agency's rules for reporting and replacement. Do not assume that continuing to use information from the missing credential is acceptable.

Stolen ID

If you believe someone took the credential, document the circumstances and follow the agency's instructions. Consider whether a police report or other notification is appropriate for your situation.

Damaged ID

A damaged credential may still contain readable information but may not be accepted if key details, security features or the photograph are compromised. Check the issuing agency's replacement rules.

Unknown location

If you are unsure whether the ID is lost or stolen, treat it cautiously until you determine its status and confirm what the issuing agency recommends.

How to replace a lost or stolen driver's license

The replacement process is controlled by the state that issued the driver's license. States can offer different replacement methods, including online, by mail or in person.

Identify the issuing state

Start with the state that issued the missing credential. A different state's motor vehicle agency generally cannot replace another state's license.

Check the official replacement page

Look for the issuing agency's current instructions, including eligibility for online replacement and any identification requirements.

Confirm what you need

Check whether you need your license number, Social Security information, address, identity documents or other information to complete the replacement.

Pay the replacement fee

Replacement fees vary by state and transaction. Some situations may have different fees or requirements.

Complete the replacement

Follow the agency's approved process and keep any confirmation or temporary credential provided to you.

Check the new credential

When you receive the replacement, confirm that the name, date of birth, address and other important information are correct.

What documents might be required?

There is no universal replacement-document list. Some states may allow a replacement with information already on file, while others may require additional identity verification.

Depending on the credential and circumstances, an agency may ask for information such as:

  • Existing or previous license information
  • Full legal name and date of birth
  • Social Security-related information where applicable
  • Proof of identity
  • Proof of residency or address
  • Additional status documentation in specific circumstances

What happens to the old ID after replacement?

The issuing agency determines the status of the old credential. In many replacement situations, the previous credential may no longer be valid once a replacement is issued.

Do not assume that a previously issued card remains valid simply because you later find it. If you recover an ID after requesting a replacement, check with the issuing agency before attempting to use it.

Important: Keep the replacement confirmation or temporary credential if the agency provides one. It may contain instructions about when the new credential becomes valid and what to do with the old card.

What if your ID was stolen?

A stolen ID can create a broader issue than a missing piece of plastic. The credential contains identifying information that may be useful to someone attempting to impersonate its owner.

After contacting the issuing agency, consider what other information was exposed. If your wallet or bag was stolen, for example, other cards, financial information or personal documents may also need attention.

If you suspect identity theft: Document what happened and use the appropriate official identity-theft or financial-institution channels for the type of information that may have been compromised.

What if your Social Security card was also lost?

A Social Security card is different from a driver's license or state ID and is handled by a different federal agency. If multiple documents were lost together, deal with each credential through its issuing authority rather than assuming one replacement process covers everything.

Be cautious about giving your Social Security number to people or websites that do not have a legitimate reason to request it.

Lost ID while traveling

If your ID disappears while you are away from home, the first step is usually to contact the agency that issued the credential and determine whether a replacement or temporary document can be obtained remotely.

If you are traveling by air or have another immediate identification problem, check the current requirements of the relevant transportation or government authority. Do not rely on an old online article for time-sensitive travel requirements.

Can you still use a photocopy or photo of your ID?

A photograph or photocopy can help you remember the information printed on the original credential, but it is not automatically a substitute for the physical government-issued ID.

Whether a copy is accepted depends entirely on the organization and purpose. For an official replacement process, follow the issuing agency's instructions rather than assuming a copy will satisfy an identity requirement.

Be careful with replacement-ID offers online

Searching for a replacement ID online can produce commercial websites that are not connected to a state motor vehicle agency. This is particularly important when the missing document is a driver's license or state ID because a commercial product cannot create the legal driving or identification status granted by a state government.

FakeIDs.com, for example, describes its products as novelty identification cards and states that they are not government-issued identification. That makes them different from the official replacement process for a lost or stolen driver's license or state ID.

If you need a legitimate replacement credential, use the issuing state's official agency. A novelty, replica or prop product should never be presented as a government-issued credential.

How to reduce the risk of identity misuse

After losing an ID, think beyond the replacement card. The appropriate precautions depend on what was lost and what information was exposed.

  • Review financial accounts if payment cards were also lost
  • Change credentials if passwords or authentication information were exposed
  • Watch for unexpected account activity or unfamiliar applications
  • Keep records of reports, replacement requests and confirmations
  • Use official identity-theft resources if you believe your information has been misused

Do not send copies of identity documents to an unfamiliar website simply because it promises a faster replacement.

Common mistakes after losing an ID

  • Waiting too long to contact the issuing agency
  • Assuming the replacement process is the same in every state
  • Using an old credential after receiving a replacement without checking its status
  • Sharing sensitive personal information with unofficial services
  • Assuming a photograph of an ID is legally equivalent to the original
  • Ignoring other documents that were lost at the same time
  • Failing to keep confirmation of a replacement request

Frequently asked questions

Can I replace my driver's license online?

Some states allow eligible residents to request a replacement online. Others require an in-person visit or impose conditions on online replacement. Check the issuing state's current procedure.

Do I need to report a lost driver's license to the police?

Not every lost license requires a police report. A stolen credential or suspected identity theft may involve different considerations. Follow the issuing agency's instructions and local requirements.

Can someone use my lost driver's license?

A missing credential may expose personal information and could potentially be misused. The appropriate response depends on what happened and what other information was exposed.

What if I find my old license after ordering a replacement?

Do not automatically assume it is still valid. Check with the issuing agency about the status of the old credential before using it.

Can I use a novelty ID as a replacement?

No. A novelty or replica ID is not a replacement for a government-issued driver's license or state identification card.

How do I find the replacement rules for my state?

Start with the state directory on US ID Laws, then verify the replacement procedure directly with the state's motor vehicle or identification agency.

Replacement rules are state-specific. Use the state directory to find the relevant information, then confirm the current instructions with the issuing government agency.
